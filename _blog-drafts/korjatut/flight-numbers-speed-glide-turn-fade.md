Every disc golf disc has four numbers printed on its top, for example 8 / 5 / -1 / 2. They tell you how the disc flies. Once you can read them, you stop guessing which disc to throw and know what is happening in your hand. Let's go through them one at a time from a right-hand backhand (RHBH) point of view.

## Speed (1–14)

Speed tells you how hard you need to throw a disc for it to fly as designed. Higher numbers need more arm power and are typically distance drivers. Lower numbers work even with a gentle throw. The Lucky Discs Money Shot (speed 4) is a slow approach disc in the midrange class, while the Treasure Hunt (speed 12) is a fast distance driver. Beginners should favour lower speeds, because they fly well without full power.

## Glide (1–7)

Glide describes a disc's ability to stay in the air. More glide helps a disc travel further with less power, which helps beginners. Less glide makes the flight more controlled and more predictable in wind. Glide is easy to overlook, but it affects distance more than you might expect.

## Turn (-5 to +1)

Turn describes a disc's tendency to drift right during the fast, early part of its flight. The more negative the number, the more the disc turns right. Negative turn (for example -2 or -3) makes a disc understable and easier for beginners to throw. A turn close to zero gives a straighter flight. Positive turn, like the Money Shot's +1, means an overstable disc that does not turn right at all.

## Fade (0–5)

Fade tells you how strongly a disc hooks left at the end of its flight as it slows down. Low fade (0–1) means a straighter finish, while high fade (3–5) means a strong hook to the left and a dependable landing. Fade is useful when you want a disc to come back left around a dogleg or to drop in a controlled way near the basket.

## Example: how to read a disc

The Lucky Discs Bank Robber (8 / 5 / -1 / 2) is a good example. Moderate speed means it works without extreme power, the glide is good, it turns slightly right early on and fades gently left at the finish. In practice it is a versatile, controllable fairway driver that even a beginner can learn to throw.

## The most important advice

Don't get hung up on the numbers alone. They are a guide, and the flight changes with arm power, wind and plastic. The most important thing is to throw a lot and learn how your own arm makes each disc fly. The numbers help you pick the right tool; the rest you learn on the course.
